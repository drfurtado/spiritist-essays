About the Author

None of that is what this site is about.

About The Spiritist Essays

The Spiritist Essays is a personal, non-commercial project — a place where I write, in my own time and entirely apart from my academic work, about Spiritism as codified by Allan Kardec and its dialogue with contemporary science.

I’ve long been drawn to the question of what a rigorous, evidence-engaged reading of Kardecian Spiritism looks like — one that takes Spiritism’s core claims seriously as hypotheses, without lapsing into devotional or confessional writing, and that takes contemporary research in consciousness studies, neuroscience, psychology, and survival studies just as seriously. That’s the project here: essays that put primary Kardecist and psychographed sources (Allan Kardec, André Luiz) into direct conversation with peer-reviewed scholarship, looking for genuine convergence, real tension, and open questions — not apologetics.

Topics I return to include:

  • Consciousness and personal identity
  • The spiritual body (perispirit) and models of embodiment
  • Survival of death and continuity of consciousness
  • Near-death and other anomalous experiences
  • Moral development and ethical progress
  • Philosophy of mind and dual-aspect theories
  • Clinical, psychological, or neuroscientific research that bears on Spiritist claims

This isn’t peer-reviewed scholarship, and it doesn’t claim to be. It’s one person’s ongoing blog, written as carefully and honestly as I can manage, with full citations so you can go check the sources yourself and disagree with me.

What Spiritism Is — and What It Is Not

Spiritism is widely misunderstood, even in Brazil, where it has its largest following. Since everything on this site depends on what the word means, it is worth stating plainly, in Kardec’s own terms. Translations from Kardec’s French are my own.

A new word for a new thing

Kardec coined the word. The Spirits’ Book (1857) opens with the principle that “new things call for new words.” The word spiritualism, Kardec explained, already had a settled meaning: any view opposed to materialism. One can be a spiritualist in that sense without believing that spirits exist or communicate with us. He therefore introduced the terms spirite and spiritisme for a doctrine whose subject is precisely the relationship between the material world and the world of Spirits (Kardec, 1857).

Kardec’s definition

In What Is Spiritism? (1859), Kardec defined it this way:

“Spiritism is at once a science of observation and a philosophical doctrine. As a practical science, it consists in the relations that can be established with Spirits; as a philosophical doctrine, it comprises all the moral consequences that follow from those relations.” (Kardec, 1859)

More formally, he called it “a science that deals with the nature, origin, and destiny of Spirits, and with their relations with the corporeal world” (Kardec, 1859).

Three terms recur throughout the fundamental works, and each has a specific meaning:

  • Science. Kardec presented Spiritism as an inquiry into a class of phenomena, mediumistic communications, studied by observation, comparison, and agreement among independent sources. He also held that it must correct itself when the evidence requires: Spiritism, he wrote, “will never be overtaken” by progress, because “if new discoveries showed it to be in error on any point, it would change on that point” (Kardec, 1868b).
  • Philosophy. The title page of The Spirits’ Book describes it as spiritualist philosophy. Its subject matter is the questions philosophy has always asked (God, the soul, the purpose of life, the moral law, what follows death), answered from the communications Kardec collected and compared (Kardec, 1857).
  • Religious consequences. A philosophy that addresses God, the soul, and the moral law cannot avoid bearing on religion. Kardec wrote that Spiritism “has moral consequences, like all philosophical sciences,” and that it leads “essentially to religious ideas” (Kardec, 1859). The Gospel According to Spiritism develops that moral side through the teachings of Jesus, read as ethics rather than dogma, and argues that science and religion, once each stops treating its own viewpoint as exclusive, are bound to meet (Kardec, 1864).

In the Brazilian Spiritist movement this is often summarized as the “threefold aspect” of Spiritism: science, philosophy, and religion. The summary is useful, but it is a later shorthand rather than Kardec’s own formula, and Kardec qualified the word religion carefully.

Is Spiritism a religion?

Kardec answered this directly in a speech delivered in Paris on November 1, 1868, and published in the Revue Spirite (Kardec, 1868a). His answer was yes and no.

In the philosophical sense, meaning a bond that unites people around shared moral convictions, as when we speak of “the religion of friendship” or “the religion of the family,” he said that “Spiritism is a religion, and we take pride in it.”

But he explained why he had nonetheless declared that Spiritism is not a religion: “there is only one word to express two different ideas,” and in common usage “the word religion is inseparable from that of worship.” In that ordinary sense, Spiritism “has none of the characteristics of a religion”: no temples, no priesthood, no ceremonies.

That is the sense in which I use the word on this site. Spiritism is a philosophy grounded in observation, with moral and religious consequences. It is not a religion in the institutional sense. Kardec also insisted that whatever faith it produces must answer to reason: “The only unshakable faith is the one that can look reason in the face, in every age of humanity” (Kardec, 1864).

What Spiritism is not

  • Not a church. It has no clergy, sacraments, rituals, vestments, altars, sacred images, or obligatory ceremonies (Kardec, 1868a). Spiritist centers typically meet for study, lectures, and charitable work.
  • Not Spiritualism. Anglo-American Spiritualism, which emerged in the United States in the late 1840s, shares an interest in mediumship but developed independently and, for the most part, did not adopt reincarnation, which is central to Kardec’s doctrine. Kardec chose a different word precisely to avoid this confusion (Kardec, 1857).
  • Not Umbanda, Candomblé, or other Afro-Brazilian religions. In Brazil, the word espírita is sometimes used loosely to cover these traditions. They have their own histories, rites, and practices, such as offerings, initiations, and devotion to the orixás, none of which are part of Kardec’s doctrine. This is a distinction, not a judgment of those traditions.
  • Not fortune-telling, magic, or the occult. Spiritism offers no divination, spells, amulets, or paid consultations. Its principle, taken from the Gospel, is “give freely what you have received freely”: mediumship is not to be sold (Kardec, 1864).
  • Not a demand for conversion. In Kardec’s words, Spiritism “imposes itself on no one” (Kardec, 1859). People of any religion, or none, can study it and accept or reject its conclusions.
  • Not a closed system. By Kardec’s own standard, a Spiritist claim that conflicts with well-established evidence must give way (Kardec, 1868b). That standard is the reason this site exists.
